# Introduction

Numerous studies have indicated a decline in cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) over the years. This study aims to examine whether this decline in CRF directly affects running performance, particularly in long-distance events. By analyzing running performance across different percentiles (90th, 50th, and 10th), this research seeks to offer insights into how CRF decline may influence performance in various competitive levels. Using data from major road race Tely 10, spanning 1995 to 2020, this study examines running speeds and calculates predicted VO₂max as a performance indicator.


# Data Collection

This technique allows for the efficient gathering of large volumes of data from websites. Web scraping can capture all data from a website or target specific information as needed by the user. Some websites explicitly prohibit users from scraping data. This is because scraping can slow down the website or potentially overload the server, causing the website to become unresponsive.

Over recent decades, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) has declined significantly in the general population, a trend attributed to reduced physical activity, increased sedentary behavior, and rising obesity rates. CRF is a critical indicator of health, with low levels associated with elevated risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality (Kodama et al., 2009). Longitudinal studies, such as the Aerobics Center Longitudinal Study, have shown that declining fitness levels correspond with increased mortality, even in previously healthy individuals (Blair et al., 1995). Surveillance data from national health programs have further supported these findings. Among youth, global trends demonstrate a notable reduction in aerobic capacity over the last several decades, reflecting broader lifestyle shifts (Tomkinson et al., 2018). Collectively, this body of research underscores the urgency of addressing the long-term decline in CRF to improve public health outcomes.

This mirrors the trends observed in epidemiological studies, which have consistently documented a reduction in population-level CRF over the past several decades. For instance, Tomkinson et al. Analyzing road race data such as from the Tely 10 provides valuable insights into population fitness trends, but it comes with several limitations. First, there is a strong self-selection bias, as only individuals who are sufficiently motivated, trained, or have the means to participate are represented, potentially overestimating average cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) relative to the general population. Second, environmental variability—including changes in weather, temperature, wind, and course conditions across years—can influence performance outcomes, reducing consistency in trend interpretation. Third, the absence of direct physiological measurements such as VO₂max, heart rate, or the precision of CRF estimates, which must instead rely on finishing times as indirect proxies. Additionally, demographic shifts over time—such as an increase in recreational runners or changes in age and gender composition—can influence race results independently of actual changes in CRF. Finally, data quality issues, especially for older races, may include missing, inconsistent, or incomplete records, introducing uncertainty into longitudinal analyses. Despite these limitations, road race data remains a practical and scalable model for estimating long-term fitness trends when interpreted with appropriate caution.
